What Is a Teaser Bet? How Teasers Work, Pay and Push

Key takeaways

  • A teaser is a parlay where every spread or total moves in your favor by a fixed number of points, usually 6, 6.5 or 7 in football and 4, 4.5 or 5 in basketball.
  • Every leg must cover its teased line, and the payout comes from a fixed table, not the legs' original odds.
  • Prices vary by book: DraftKings' Massachusetts rules list -135 for a 2-team, 6-point NFL teaser, which needs each leg to cover about 75.8% of the time.
  • Wong teasers cross both NFL key numbers, 3 and 7, by teasing favorites of -7.5 to -8.5 and underdogs of +1.5 to +2.5.
  • Push rules differ: a pushed leg can drop the teaser a level, make it no action or lose it, depending on the book and teaser type.

A teaser bet is a parlay where you move the point spread or total in your favor on every leg, in exchange for a lower payout. So what is a teaser bet in practice? You might turn a -7.5 favorite into a -1.5 favorite and a +2.5 underdog into a +8.5 underdog, but both legs still have to cover the new numbers for you to get paid.

Teasers are offered on football and basketball at most legal US sportsbooks, and they are especially popular during the NFL season. This guide explains how teasers work, how much they pay, what happens when a leg pushes, and how to check whether a teaser price is worth taking. You can follow along with the free Teaser Calculator.

Quick answer: A teaser bet combines two or more football or basketball spreads or totals into one parlay, and each line is moved a fixed number of points in your favor, usually 6, 6.5 or 7 in football and 4, 4.5 or 5 in basketball. Every leg must win. In return for the better lines, the payout is far lower than a normal parlay.

How does a teaser bet work?

A teaser works like a parlay with one big change: before the bet is graded, the sportsbook shifts every line in your favor by the number of points you chose. Favorites give fewer points, underdogs get more points, overs get a lower total and unders get a higher total.

  1. Pick the teaser type. Choose the sport and the number of points, such as a 6-point NFL teaser.
  2. Add your legs. Most books require at least two legs. DraftKings allows 2 to 6 teams on a regular teaser, while Caesars allows two to eight events, according to their DraftKings and Caesars Massachusetts house rules filed with the state regulator.
  3. The lines move. Every leg moves by the same amount. DraftKings, for example, does not allow mixed point adjustments in one teaser.
  4. Every leg must cover. If any leg loses against its teased line, the whole teaser loses.
  5. You are paid at the teaser price. The odds come from a fixed table based on the number of legs and points, not from the individual game odds.

That last point is the key difference from a parlay. In a parlay, each leg's odds multiply together. In a teaser, the book uses a set price, such as -135 for a 2-team, 6-point NFL teaser, whatever the original prices of the legs were.

What does teasing mean in betting?

"Teasing" a line means moving it in your favor. If you tease the Chiefs -7.5 by 6 points, you now have the Chiefs -1.5. If you tease an over 44.5 by 6 points, you now have over 38.5. When bettors say they "teased" a team, they mean they used that team as a leg in a teaser.

What does a teaser bet look like? A worked example

Say you place a 2-team, 6-point NFL teaser for $100 with these two legs:

LegOriginal lineTeased line (+6)Final scoreResult
Team A (favorite)-7.5-1.5Team A wins 24-20Wins by 4, covers -1.5
Team B (underdog)+2.5+8.5Team B loses 27-21Loses by 6, covers +8.5

Against the original lines, you would have gone 0-2: Team A did not win by 8 or more, and Team B lost by more than 2.5. With the teased lines, both legs cover, so the teaser wins.

At DraftKings' posted price of -135 for a 2-team, 6-point NFL teaser, a $100 bet returns $174.07 in total, which is $74.07 profit. For comparison, a normal 2-team parlay at -110 on each leg would pay $264.46 profit on the same $100, but both legs would have needed to cover the original, harder lines.

How much does a teaser bet pay?

Teaser payouts depend on three things: the sport, the number of points and the number of legs. More points make each leg easier, so the price drops. More legs make the bet harder, so the price rises. There is no single industry table, and the posted odds vary by sportsbook and state.

Here is one real example. These are the regular NFL teaser odds in DraftKings' Massachusetts sportsbook house rules (effective October 7, 2026), with the profit on a $100 bet calculated from each price:

Legs6 points6.5 points7 pointsProfit on $100 (6 points)
2 teams-135-143-150$74.07
3 teams+140+130+120$140.00
4 teams+240+200+180$240.00
5 teams+333+290+250$333.00
6 teams+500+400+350$500.00

The same document lists different tables for other sports. A 2-team basketball teaser is -110 at 4 points, -120 at 4.5 points and -130 at 5 points. A 2-team, 6-point college football teaser is -115, a better price than the NFL version. Caesars' Massachusetts rules, by contrast, do not print a table at all and say teaser odds are shown when you place the bet and can change at any time.

The takeaway: always check the teaser price on your bet slip, and compare books if you can. A 2-team, 6-point NFL teaser at -110 and the same teaser at -135 are very different bets, as the next section shows.

What win rate does a teaser need to break even?

Because every leg must win, you can work out how often each leg has to cover for the teaser to break even. Convert the teaser price to an implied probability, then take the square root for two legs (or the cube root for three legs). The Implied Probability Calculator does the first step for you.

Teaser priceLegsImplied probabilityEach leg must cover
-110252.4%72.4%
-120254.5%73.9%
-130256.5%75.2%
-135257.4%75.8%
-150260.0%77.5%
+140341.7%74.7%

Worked example for -135: the decimal price is 1.7407, so the implied probability is 1 / 1.7407 = 57.4%. The square root of 0.574 is about 0.758, so each leg needs to cover its teased line about 75.8% of the time (assuming the two games are independent).

That is a high bar. In an Action Network analysis of every regular-season NFL spread since 2003 teased by 6 points, teams covered only 68.7% of the time (6,638-3,026). Teasing a random line usually loses money at these prices.

What is a Wong teaser?

A Wong teaser, also called a basic strategy teaser, is a 6-point NFL teaser that moves each leg across both of football's key numbers, 3 and 7. It is named after gambling author Stanford Wong. In practice that means teasing:

  • Favorites of -7.5 to -8.5 down to -1.5 to -2.5
  • Underdogs of +1.5 to +2.5 up to +7.5 to +8.5

Why those numbers? NFL scoring comes in 3s and 7s, so final margins bunch up there. According to Boyd's Bets data on NFL games since 1989, 15.39% of games were decided by exactly 3 points and 8.74% by exactly 7. A 6-point tease from +2.5 to +8.5 picks up both of those margins, which is far more valuable than moving from, say, +10 to +16, where few games land.

The same Action Network study found that underdogs from +1.5 to +2.5 covered a 6-point teaser 75.2% of the time (374-123) and favorites from -7.5 to -8.5 covered 76.8% of the time (218-66), or 75.8% combined. Notice that 75.8% is almost exactly the break-even rate at -135. Historical results are not a promise of future results, and the price matters: the same study uses -110 as its baseline and warns that paying more than -130 tilts the edge heavily toward the book. Can a teaser bet push?

Yes. A leg can land exactly on its teased number, for example a team teased to -3 that wins by exactly 3. What happens next depends on the book's house rules, so check them before you bet. Here is how two major US sportsbooks handle it in their Massachusetts rules:

  • DraftKings, regular teasers: if a leg pushes or is voided, the teaser "will drop to the next level down." A 3-team, 6-point NFL teaser becomes a 2-team teaser and is paid at -135 instead of +140. If a 2-team teaser has one push or void and no losing leg, it is graded no action and your stake is refunded.
  • DraftKings, Super and Monster teasers: for NFL and college football versions, any tie or push is settled as a loss.
  • Caesars: if a teaser is reduced to one live leg by a push or void, a winning leg is paid at the "straight wager teaser price," according to its Massachusetts house rules.

Rules can even differ between states for the same brand: Action Network notes that Caesars in Las Vegas has refunded the whole bet when one leg of a 2-team teaser pushes and the other wins, unlike Caesars elsewhere. Read the teaser section of the house rules where you bet.

Can you tease totals and other markets?

Most books let you tease full-game point spreads and totals in football and basketball. Caesars' rules say teasers can only be placed on main, full-game spread and total markets, and DraftKings limits teasers to pre-match basketball and football games. You generally cannot tease moneylines, player props or live lines.

Teasing a total works the same way: over 44.5 teased 6 points becomes over 38.5, and under 44.5 becomes under 50.5. Totals do not cluster on key numbers the way NFL margins do, so a 6-point move on a total usually captures fewer outcomes than a well-chosen spread tease. Action Network's analysis advises against teasing totals in general. If you are new to totals, start with our guide to what over/under means in betting.

Teaser vs parlay: what is the difference?

Both bets combine multiple legs that must all win. The difference is the trade-off between line and price.

FeatureTeaserParlay
LinesMoved in your favor by a fixed amountStandard lines
MarketsUsually football and basketball spreads and totals onlyMost markets, including moneylines and props
PriceFixed table by legs and pointsEach leg's odds multiplied together
2 legs at -110, $100 stake$74.07 profit at -135 (6-point NFL, DraftKings MA)$264.46 profit
Push on a legDrops a level or no action, depending on the bookLeg removed and odds recalculated at most books

Neither bet is better by default. A parlay pays more because it is harder to win. A teaser pays less because it is easier. What matters is whether the price is fair for the extra points you receive. For more on parlays, see our parlay betting tips.

Common teaser mistakes to avoid

  • Teasing through zero. Moving a -3 favorite to +3 sounds great, but it wastes points on margins near zero, where few NFL games finish. Action Network specifically warns against teases that cross zero.
  • Ignoring the price. At -110 each 2-team leg needs about 72.4% to break even; at -150 it needs about 77.5%. Small price changes matter a lot.
  • Adding too many legs. Every leg you add multiplies the chance that one loses. Bigger teasers pay more, but they need a high cover rate on every leg.
  • Using basketball like football. The key-number research behind Wong teasers is based on NFL scoring, so it does not carry over to basketball, where points come in 1s, 2s and 3s.
  • Not reading push rules. A push can drop your teaser a level, refund it or lose it, depending on the book and teaser type.

How do you calculate a teaser bet?

You can calculate any teaser in four steps:

  1. Add the teaser points to each underdog spread, subtract them from each favorite spread, lower each over total and raise each under total.
  2. Find the teaser price for your number of legs and points on the book's table or bet slip.
  3. Convert the price to decimal odds. For negative odds, decimal = 1 + 100 / odds (so -135 = 1.7407). For positive odds, decimal = 1 + odds / 100 (so +140 = 2.40). Our Odds Converter does this instantly.
  4. Multiply your stake by the decimal odds for the total return, then subtract the stake for profit. $50 on a 3-team teaser at +140 returns $120.00, which is $70.00 profit.

The Teaser Calculator handles the line moves and payout in one place. Its built-in odds are a general guide, so if your book shows a different price, use the price on your bet slip.

A teaser is a simple idea: better lines for a smaller payout. Whether it makes sense comes down to the price your book offers and which numbers your legs cross.
