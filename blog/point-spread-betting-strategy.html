In the world of sports wagering, the Point Spread Betting Strategy is the most common and arguably the most nuanced way to bet on team sports like football and basketball. While the moneyline simply asks 'who will win?', the spread asks 'by how much?' and 'will they cover?'

What is Point Spread Betting?

Point spread betting, often referred to as 'handicapping,' is a method used by oddsmakers to level the playing field between two teams of unequal skill. The sportsbook assigns a point handicap to the favorite (the team expected to win) and a point advantage to the underdog (the team expected to lose).

The goal for the bookmaker is to attract equal betting volume on both sides, ensuring they collect their 'vig' or commission regardless of the outcome. For the bettor, the challenge is to determine if the oddsmakers have accurately assessed the margin of victory.

How the Point Spread Works

When you look at a betting board, you will see a plus (+) or minus (-) sign next to a number. These represent the spread:

  • The Favorite (-): A negative number means the team must win by more than that amount to 'cover the spread.' For example, if the Kansas City Chiefs are -7, they must win by 8 or more points for a bet on them to pay out.
  • The Underdog (+): A positive number means the team can win the game outright OR lose by less than that amount to cover. If the Las Vegas Raiders are +7, you win if they win the game or lose by 6 points or fewer.

If the game ends exactly on the number (e.g., Chiefs win by exactly 7), the result is a 'push,' and your original stake is refunded.

The Math of ATS (Against the Spread)

In professional betting circles, you'll often hear the term ATS (Against the Spread). A team's ATS record tracks how they perform relative to the oddsmakers' expectations, not just their win-loss record. A team might be 10-0 straight up but only 4-6 ATS if they consistently fail to win by as much as the market expects.

Because most spread bets carry odds of -110, you are risking $110 to win $100. This built-in house edge means you must win 52.38% of your bets just to break even. Key Numbers: The Secret to NFL Spreads

One of the most critical components of a successful Point Spread Betting Strategy, particularly in the NFL, is understanding key numbers. NFL games frequently end with a margin of victory of 3, 7, or 10 points due to the nature of scoring (field goals and touchdowns).

About 15% of NFL games end with a 3-point margin, and roughly 10% end with a 7-point margin. This makes the difference between a spread of -2.5 and -3.5 massive. Professional bettors will fight tooth and nail to get a favorite at -2.5 or an underdog at +3.5 to 'capture' the key number of 3.

The 'Hook' and Avoiding the Push

The '.5' added to a spread is known as the 'hook.' Its primary purpose is to eliminate the possibility of a push. If you bet on a team at -3.5, you either win (if they win by 4+) or lose (if they win by 3 or less). There is no middle ground.

Many bettors use a Half-Point Calculator to determine if it is mathematically worth 'buying the hook' (paying a higher vig to move a spread from -3 to -2.5). Generally, buying onto or off of the 3 or 7 is the only time this is profitable over the long term.

Line Shopping: The Easiest Way to Win

The single most underrated part of a Point Spread Betting Strategy is line shopping. Different sportsbooks have different liabilities and different 'shaping' of their lines. Bookie A might have the Dallas Cowboys at -6.5, while Bookie B has them at -7.

If you are betting on the favorite, Bookie A is the obvious choice. If you are betting the underdog, Bookie B gives you the safety of a potential push at 7. Over a season of 100 bets, getting an extra half-point on just five games can be the difference between a losing season and a profitable one.

Fading the Public and Reverse Line Movement

Recreational bettors (the 'public') love favorites and high-scoring games. This creates 'public bias,' where oddsmakers might inflate a spread to -7.5 for a popular team like the Lakers when the statistical 'fair' line is -6.

Fading the public means betting the opposite of the majority. A key indicator of this is Reverse Line Movement (RLM). If 70% of the bets are on the favorite at -7, but the line moves down to -6.5, it tells you that 'sharp' money (professional bettors) is hammering the underdog, forcing the bookie to adjust despite the public's preference.

Spread vs. Moneyline: Making the Right Choice

Should you bet the spread or the moneyline? It depends on the price. If you think a +7 underdog is going to win the game outright, the moneyline (often +250 or higher) offers a much better payout than the spread (-110). However, if you think the game will be a 'nail-biter,' the spread provides the insurance of winning even if they lose by a touchdown.

Advanced Strategy: Teasers and Correlated Parlays

For those looking for more complexity, Teasers allow you to shift the spread in your favor (usually by 6 points in the NFL) in exchange for parlaying two or more games together. The famous 'Wong Teaser' involves crossing the key numbers of 3 and 7 (e.g., teasing a -7.5 favorite down to -1.5 or a +1.5 underdog up to +7.5).
