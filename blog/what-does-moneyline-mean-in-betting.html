What Does Moneyline Mean in Betting? How to Read Odds and Payouts

In betting, the moneyline is a bet on which team or player wins the game outright, with no point spread involved. The favorite carries a minus number (like -150) that shows how much you risk to win $100, and the underdog carries a plus number (like +130) that shows how much a $100 bet wins.

That is the whole idea behind the moneyline meaning: pick the winner, and the odds decide how much you get paid. It is the simplest bet on any sportsbook menu, which is why it's the first line you see next to the spread and the total for every NFL, NBA, NHL, MLB and college game.

This guide shows you how to read moneyline odds, work out exactly what a bet pays, turn the odds into a win probability, and handle the tricky cases: ties, overtime, 3-way lines and moneyline parlays. You can check every number with the free Moneyline Calculator.

Quick answer: A moneyline bet means you're picking the outright winner of a game. Negative odds (-150) mark the favorite and show what you must risk to win $100. Positive odds (+130) mark the underdog and show what a $100 bet wins. If your team wins by any margin, you get paid at those odds.

What does moneyline mean in betting?

A moneyline (also written "money line" or shortened to "ML") is a straight bet on the winner. Caesars Sportsbook's glossary defines it as "a wager on which team will win the game outright, with no point spread involved" (Caesars sports betting glossary). The margin of victory doesn't matter. A one-point win and a 30-point win pay exactly the same.

Because the favorite is more likely to win, the sportsbook pays less for it. Because the underdog is less likely to win, it pays more. The odds are how the book balances that difference. A typical line looks like this:

  • Team A -150 (favorite): risk $150 to win $100.
  • Team B +130 (underdog): risk $100 to win $130.

You don't have to bet $100 or $150. Those numbers are just the scale the odds are written on. Any stake pays in the same proportion.

How do you read moneyline odds?

Moneyline odds are American odds, so the sign tells you which side is which. If you want a full refresher on the plus and minus signs themselves, see what plus and minus mean in betting. Here's the short version for moneylines:

  1. Find the minus number. That team is the favorite. The number is what you risk to win $100.
  2. Find the plus number. That team is the underdog. The number is what you win on a $100 bet.
  3. Check how far the numbers are from 100. The further from 100, the bigger the favorite or underdog. -110 is close to a coin flip; -300 is a heavy favorite.
  4. Remember your stake comes back. A winning bet returns your stake plus the profit.

When both sides are close to even, you may see both teams at a minus price, such as -110 and -110, or -115 and -105. That means the book sees the game as nearly 50/50 and is charging its fee on both sides. Some books also call an even game a "pick'em."

How much does a moneyline bet pay?

The math is simple once you know the two formulas:

  • Negative odds: profit = stake × (100 ÷ odds). At -150, a $50 bet wins $50 × (100 ÷ 150) = $33.33.
  • Positive odds: profit = stake × (odds ÷ 100). At +130, a $50 bet wins $50 × 1.30 = $65.00.

Worked example: betting the favorite vs the underdog

Say a game is posted at Team A -150 and Team B +130, and you have $50 to bet.

  • $50 on Team A (-150): if Team A wins, you profit $33.33 and get $83.33 back. If Team A loses, you lose $50.
  • $50 on Team B (+130): if Team B wins, you profit $65.00 and get $115.00 back. If Team B loses, you lose $50.

The favorite wins more often but pays less; the underdog pays more but wins less often. Neither side is automatically "better." What matters is whether the price is fair compared with the real chance of winning, which is where implied probability comes in.

What do moneyline odds say about a team's chances?

Every moneyline can be turned into an implied probability: the win chance the price assumes. The formulas are:

  • Negative odds: odds ÷ (odds + 100). For -150: 150 ÷ 250 = 60.0%.
  • Positive odds: 100 ÷ (odds + 100). For +130: 100 ÷ 230 = 43.5%.

Add the two sides together and you get 103.5%, not 100%. The extra 3.5% is the sportsbook's built-in margin, called the vig or juice. Remove it proportionally and the "fair" no-vig chances are about 58.0% for Team A and 42.0% for Team B. Our guide to what vig is and how it's calculated walks through this step by step.

Moneyline vs spread: what's the difference?

A point spread bet asks whether a team wins by more than a set number of points (or loses by fewer). A moneyline bet only asks who wins. That one difference changes the price and the risk.

Example: a favorite might be -150 on the moneyline and -3 (-110) on the spread. If it wins by exactly 3, the moneyline bet wins and the spread bet pushes. If it wins by 1 or 2, the moneyline bet wins and the spread bet loses. The flip side: a $110 spread bet at -110 profits $100, while a $110 moneyline bet at -150 profits only $73.33.

Baseball and hockey are mostly bet on the moneyline because games are low-scoring; their spread versions (the run line and puck line, usually set at 1.5) are explained in what -1.5 means in betting. What happens to a moneyline bet in a tie or overtime?

Settlement rules are set by each sportsbook's house rules, which state regulators approve, so always check your book. The common pattern, using DraftKings' Massachusetts house rules as an example (DraftKings house rules, Massachusetts Gaming Commission):

  • Overtime counts on a standard (2-way) moneyline. DraftKings says bets on 2-way moneyline markets "include results from any overtime and/or shootout." So an NBA or NFL game decided in overtime, or an NHL game decided in a shootout, pays the winner.
  • A tie in a 2-way market is a push. If there's no "tie" option and the game ends level, the bet is settled as a push and your stake is returned.
  • 3-way moneylines use regulation time only. These markets include a draw option and do not count overtime or shootouts.

Ties are rare but real in the NFL. In the regular season, a game tied after one 15-minute overtime period "will be recorded as a tie," while postseason games cannot end in a tie (NFL Football Operations overtime rules). So a regular-season NFL moneyline can push; a playoff moneyline can't.

Baseball moneylines

Baseball games can be shortened by weather. Under DraftKings' rules, an MLB moneyline stands once the game is official (generally after five innings, or four and a half if the home team leads) even if nine innings aren't completed, extra innings count, and a change of starting pitcher does not void a moneyline bet on its own. Some books offer "listed pitcher" options that do void if a named starter doesn't pitch, so rules here vary more than in other sports.

Postponed games

If a game is postponed or suspended and not finished within the book's window (DraftKings uses two calendar days in most cases), moneyline bets are usually voided and stakes returned.

What is a 3-way moneyline?

A 3-way moneyline adds a third outcome: the draw. It's the standard way soccer is bet, and books also offer it for hockey (often called the "60-minute line" or "regulation line") and sometimes for football and basketball. You pick home win, draw or away win, settled on regulation time only.

Example soccer line: Home +140, Draw +240, Away +200. A $50 bet pays $70.00 profit on the home side, $120.00 on the draw, or $100.00 on the away side. The implied probabilities are 41.7%, 29.4% and 33.3%, which add up to 104.4%: the extra is the book's margin, spread across three outcomes.

The key difference from a 2-way line: if you bet a hockey team on the 3-way line and the game goes to overtime, your bet loses even if your team wins in overtime, because the regulation result was a draw. That's why 3-way prices on both teams are higher than their 2-way prices. The moneyline betting strategy guide covers when bettors compare the two.

What does ML mean in betting?

"ML" (sometimes typed "M/L" or "m l") is short for moneyline. If someone posts "Bills ML -150," they mean a moneyline bet on the Bills at -150 odds. You'll see a few related shorthands on bet slips and social media:

  • ML parlay: a parlay made up of moneyline legs.
  • 1H ML / 1Q ML: a moneyline on just the first half or first quarter. These are often 3-way markets because a half can end tied.
  • Live ML: an in-game moneyline whose odds update as the score changes.
  • PK or pick'em: a game with no favorite on the spread; on the moneyline both sides are priced close to even.

More shorthand is explained in our plain-English sports betting terms glossary.

How do moneyline parlays work?

A moneyline parlay combines two or more moneyline picks into one bet. Every leg must win, and the payout is the product of each leg's decimal odds.

Example: a $20 parlay with legs at -150 (1.667), +130 (2.30) and -200 (1.50). Multiply 1.667 × 2.30 × 1.50 = 5.75. The parlay returns $115.00, a profit of $95.00. If one leg loses, the whole parlay loses.

If one leg pushes (say an NFL game ends in a tie), most books drop that leg and recalculate the parlay on the remaining legs, which DraftKings' house rules confirm. In this example, if the +130 leg pushed, the bet becomes a two-leg parlay at 1.667 × 1.50 = 2.50, returning $50.00 on your $20. Common moneyline mistakes to avoid

  • Treating heavy favorites as "free money." At -300 you need to win 75% of the time just to break even. One loss wipes out the profit from three wins.
  • Forgetting the stake scale. -150 doesn't mean you must bet $150. It's a ratio; $15 at -150 wins $10.
  • Mixing up 2-way and 3-way lines. In soccer and in hockey's 60-minute line, a draw after regulation loses a team bet, even if the team wins in overtime.
  • Ignoring the price difference between books. -140 and -150 on the same team are different bets. Over many bets, small price gaps add up.
  • Assuming every book settles the same way. Ties, shortened baseball games and postponements follow each book's house rules, which vary by operator and state.

The moneyline is the cleanest way to bet a game: pick the winner and let the odds set the payout.
