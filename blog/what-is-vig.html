What Is Vig in Betting? How the Juice Works and How to Calculate It

Vig is the fee a sportsbook builds into the odds for taking your bet, and it is the main way a book makes money. In betting, the vig (short for vigorish, also called juice) is why you usually risk $110 to win $100 on a point spread instead of risking $100 to win $100.

You never see the vig as a separate charge on your bet slip. It hides inside the price, so the only way to see it is to do a little math. This guide shows you exactly what vig is, how to calculate it from any pair of odds, how to strip it out to get no-vig (fair) odds, and why a few cents of juice decides whether a bettor can come out ahead over a season.

What is vig in betting?

Vig is the sportsbook's cut. Merriam-Webster defines vigorish as "a charge taken (as by a bookie or a gambling house) on bets," and says the word perhaps comes from the Ukrainian or Russian word for winnings or profit. Its first known use in English dates to 1912. Bettors shorten it to "vig," and "juice" means the same thing.

The word has a second, non-betting meaning: interest paid to a moneylender, which is why some searches for "what is vig" turn up loan-shark stories. In sports betting, though, vig always means the price you pay the book for taking your wager.

The key idea is that the vig is not a fee added on top of your stake. It is baked into the odds. A fair coin flip should pay even money (+100). A sportsbook offers both sides at -110 instead, and that gap between +100 and -110 is the vig.

  • Vig / vigorish: the commission built into the odds.
  • Juice: slang for the same thing ("the juice on this line is -115").
  • Overround or margin: how far the implied probabilities of all outcomes add up past 100%.
  • Hold: the share of money wagered that the book actually keeps after results are in.

How does the vig work on a -110 bet?

Take an NFL point spread where both teams are listed at -110. Odds of -110 mean you risk $110 to win $100. If you win, you get back $210: your $110 stake plus $100 profit. If you lose, you lose $110.

Now picture the sportsbook taking exactly $110 on each side. Wikipedia's vigorish entry walks through this same case: the book collects $220, pays the winner $210, and keeps $10 no matter which team covers.

StepBettor A (Team 1 -110)Bettor B (Team 2 -110)Sportsbook
Money wagered$110$110Holds $220
Team 1 coversGets back $210Loses $110Keeps $10
Team 2 coversLoses $110Gets back $210Keeps $10

The book's $10 is 4.55% of the $220 wagered (10 / 220 = 0.04545). That is the vig on a -110/-110 market. In real life books rarely get perfectly balanced action, so their actual result on a game swings around, but the 4.55% is the cut built into the price.

How do you calculate vig?

You can calculate vig from any two-way or three-way market in four steps. Implied probability is the win chance the odds suggest; if you need a refresher, see understanding odds and implied probability.

  1. Convert each side to implied probability. For negative odds: odds / (odds + 100), using the number without the minus sign. For positive odds: 100 / (odds + 100).
  2. Add the implied probabilities. The total above 100% is the overround (the book's margin).
  3. Find the vig: vig = 1 - (1 / total implied probability). This is the share of money the book keeps if action is balanced.
  4. Find the fair (no-vig) probability of each side: that side's implied probability / total implied probability.

Worked example at -110/-110: each side is 110 / 210 = 52.38%. The total is 104.76%, so the overround is 4.76%. The vig is 1 - 1/1.0476 = 4.55%. Each side's fair probability is 52.38 / 104.76 = 50%, which is +100 in American odds.

Overround (4.76%) and vig (4.55%) describe the same margin from two angles. Overround is measured against the probabilities; vig is measured against the money wagered. Both are fine as long as you compare like with like.

Lines are not always symmetrical. A spread posted at -108/-112 has implied probabilities of 51.92% and 52.83%, a total of 104.75% and a vig of about 4.54%: almost the same cut as -110/-110, just shaded toward one side.

How do you find the vig on moneyline odds?

The same four steps work when the two sides have different prices. Say a moneyline is Favorite -150, Underdog +130.

  • Favorite -150: 150 / 250 = 60.00%
  • Underdog +130: 100 / 230 = 43.48%
  • Total: 103.48%, so the overround is 3.48%
  • Vig: 1 - 1/1.0348 = 3.36%

Removing the vig gives fair probabilities of 57.98% for the favorite and 42.02% for the underdog. In American odds that is about -138 and +138. So the book is shading the favorite from -138 to -150 and the dog from +138 to +130, and that shading on both sides is the juice. A wider pair such as -200/+170 works out to a vig of about 3.57% (fair odds near -180/+180).

Three-way markets (soccer)

Soccer moneylines usually have three outcomes: home, draw and away. Add all three. For example, Home +150 (40.00%), Draw +230 (30.30%) and Away +190 (34.48%) total 104.79%, a vig of about 4.57%. The fair odds would be roughly +162, +246 and +204. Three-way markets with longer prices often carry a bigger margin than this, so it pays to check.

What are no-vig odds?

No-vig odds (also called fair odds) are the prices you get after removing the sportsbook's margin. They turn the market's implied probabilities back into a total of exactly 100%. At -110/-110 the no-vig odds are +100/+100; at -150/+130 they are about -138/+138.

Bettors use no-vig odds for three things:

  • Estimating true probability. A sharp market's no-vig price is a reasonable starting estimate of the real win chance.
  • Finding value. If one book's fair price says 42% and another book offers odds that imply only 38%, that second price may have positive expected value. Our value betting strategy guide explains how to use this.
  • Comparing books. Two books can post the same spread with different juice. The fair odds tell you who is charging less.

The method above (dividing each probability by the total) is the simplest way to remove vig and the one most calculators use by default. Other methods spread the margin differently, especially between big favorites and long shots, so treat any no-vig price as an estimate, not a fact.

Is vig the same as a sportsbook's hold?

Not quite. Vig is the margin built into the odds before a game is played. Hold (or "win") is what the book actually keeps after games settle, measured as revenue divided by the total amount wagered (the handle).

Hold can be far above or below the vig on a single game because books rarely have perfectly balanced action and results swing. It also blends many markets together: spreads near -110, moneylines, props, futures and parlays, each with different margins. State regulators publish these totals. For example, the New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement reported $1.18 billion in sports wagering gross revenue for 2025, and its monthly reports list handle and win percentage alongside revenue.

For your own betting, the number that matters is the vig on the price you are taking, because that is the cost you pay on every bet.

How much vig is in a parlay?

Parlays multiply the vig along with the odds. Each leg carries its own juice, so the margin compounds as you add legs. Here is what standard -110 legs pay compared with the fair payout if every leg were a true 50/50 coin flip:

Why does the vig matter to bettors?

Vig sets the bar you must clear just to break even. At -110 you need to win 52.38% of your bets to break even, not 50%. Win exactly half and you lose money.

Here is the math on 100 spread bets at -110, risking $110 to win $100 each time, with a 50-50 record:

  • 50 wins x $100 = $5,000 profit
  • 50 losses x $110 = $5,500 lost
  • Net result: -$500 on $11,000 wagered, or -4.55%

That -4.55% is the vig showing up in your results. The juice also changes the target for every price you bet. At -105 the break-even rate drops to 51.22%; at -120 it climbs to 54.55%. Over hundreds of bets, a couple of percentage points is the difference between a winning and losing record.

How to pay less vig

  • Shop lines. Different licensed books in your state often post the same spread at different prices (-105 at one, -115 at another).
  • Look for reduced-juice markets. Some sportsbooks offer -105 or similar lines on selected markets. Availability varies by book and state.
  • Know which bets carry more juice. Parlays, teasers, props and futures usually carry a bigger margin than a standard spread. Our teaser bet guide shows how teaser payouts compare.
  • Check the expected value. Plug your estimated win chance and the price into the Expected Value Calculator to see whether the juice wipes out your edge.

What mistakes do bettors make with the vig?

  • Thinking you pay vig only when you lose. The juice is in the price either way: when you win at -110 you collect $100 instead of the fair $110.
  • Treating 52.38% as a profit target. It is the break-even point at -110, not a winning record.
  • Adding implied probabilities from different books. Calculate vig with both sides from the same book at the same time; mixing books measures something else.
  • Ignoring the juice on alternate lines and props. A -130/-110 prop has a total implied probability of 108.90% and a vig of about 8.17%, much higher than a standard spread.
  • Using hold and vig interchangeably. A book's monthly hold is not the price you paid on your bet.

The bottom line: vig is the cost of every bet you make, and it is easy to measure. Convert the odds, add them up, and you will know exactly what the book is charging. Run any line through the No-Vig Calculator to see the fair odds before you bet.
