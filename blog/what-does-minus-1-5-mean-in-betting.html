What Does Minus 1.5 Mean in Betting? +1.5 and -1.5 on the Puck Line, Run Line and More

Key takeaways

  • -1.5 means your team must win by 2 or more; +1.5 means it can lose by 1 and still win the bet.
  • The half point removes ties, so a 1.5 line can never push.
  • NHL puck lines at DraftKings and Caesars include overtime and shootouts, so any game decided after regulation goes to the +1.5 side.
  • The run line is fixed at 1.5 runs; soccer 1.5-goal handicaps and totals are usually settled on 90 minutes plus stoppage time.
  • Compare the price with the moneyline using implied probability: -1.5 at +150 needs a 40.0% win rate, +1.5 at -180 needs 64.3%.

In betting, minus 1.5 (-1.5) means the team you back must win by 2 or more; plus 1.5 (+1.5) means your team can lose by 1 and still win the bet. You will see these lines most often as the hockey puck line, the baseball run line and the soccer goal handicap, and also as "over/under 1.5" on totals and player props.

The half point matters: because no team can win by one and a half goals or runs, a 1.5 line can never push. This guide shows how each version works, how the sportsbooks settle overtime, shootouts and extra innings, and how to compare the price of a -1.5 bet with the plain moneyline using real payout math.

Quick answer: A -1.5 line means the team must win by at least 2 for your bet to win. A +1.5 line means the team wins your bet if it wins outright or loses by exactly 1. The half point removes ties, so there is no push. In hockey this is the puck line; in baseball it is the run line.

What does minus 1.5 mean in betting?

A minus sign in front of 1.5 marks the favorite on a spread. The sportsbook subtracts 1.5 from that team's final score before deciding the bet. If the Rangers are -1.5 and win 4-2, their adjusted score is 2.5 to 2, so -1.5 wins. If they win 3-2, the adjusted score is 1.5 to 2, so -1.5 loses even though the team won the game.

Put simply, -1.5 is the same as asking "will this team win by 2 or more?" That is harder than just winning, which is why a -1.5 line usually pays more than the same team's moneyline. A heavy moneyline favorite at -190 can turn into a plus-money price such as +150 at -1.5.

Do not confuse the 1.5 with the odds. A bet listed as "Rangers -1.5 (+150)" has two numbers: -1.5 is the spread (the margin), and +150 is the price (what you get paid). Our spread calculator separates the two and shows the payout for any line and price.

What does plus 1.5 mean in betting?

Plus 1.5 is the other side of the same market: the underdog gets 1.5 added to its final score. A +1.5 bet wins in two situations:

  • The team wins the game outright, by any score.
  • The team loses by exactly 1 goal or run.

It loses only if the team is beaten by 2 or more. Because that covers a lot of outcomes, +1.5 is usually priced as a favorite (for example -180) even when the team itself is the moneyline underdog. You are trading a smaller payout for a cushion of one goal or run.

So "what does 1.5 mean in betting" has a short answer: 1.5 is the margin. The plus or minus sign tells you whether your team gets that margin (+) or has to beat it (-).

Why is the line 1.5 and not 1 or 2?

Hockey, baseball and soccer are low-scoring, and many games are decided by one goal or one run. A line of exactly 1 would push (refund your stake) every time the favorite won by one. A line of 2 would push on every two-goal win. Setting the line at 1.5 splits the outcomes cleanly into "won by 2 or more" and "everything else," so every bet is decided.

That half point is called the hook. In football and basketball you also see whole-number spreads like -3 or -7 where a push is possible; DraftKings' rules, for example, settle a two-way point spread that lands exactly on the number as a push and return the stake. To see how much a half point is worth on bigger spreads, try the half point calculator.

What is the puck line in hockey betting?

The puck line is hockey's version of the point spread, and it is almost always set at 1.5 goals: the favorite at -1.5 and the underdog at +1.5. Sportsbooks adjust the price instead of the number, so two puck lines on the same night might be -1.5 (+165) and -1.5 (+120), depending on how strong the favorite is.

Do overtime and shootouts count on the puck line?

At DraftKings and Caesars, yes for NHL games. Caesars' Massachusetts house rules say overtime and shootouts are included on the puck line for North American hockey and that in a shootout "the winner is awarded one goal" (Caesars house rules). DraftKings' Massachusetts rules say hockey markets include overtime and that if a game is decided by a shootout, one goal is added to the winning team's end-of-regulation score (DraftKings house rules). The NHL itself also credits the shootout winner with one goal in its standings (NHL hockey glossary).

The practical result: any game that goes to overtime or a shootout ends with a one-goal margin, so the -1.5 side loses and the +1.5 side wins. Rules differ for some markets and leagues. Caesars, for instance, excludes overtime and shootouts for non-North American and international hockey, and both books list regulation-only three-way markets. Always check the market name and your book's house rules.

Puck line vs moneyline: what is the difference?

The moneyline asks only "who wins?" The puck line asks "who wins by 2 or more?" (or, on the +1.5 side, "who doesn't lose by 2 or more?"). Same game, different question, so the prices are very different. The table uses sample prices for one game to show how the numbers compare.

The break-even win rate is the implied probability of the odds: how often the bet has to win just to break even. Team A at -1.5 (+150) pays almost three times as much as the moneyline, but only makes sense if you believe Team A wins by 2 or more at least 40% of the time. Neither side is "better" in general; the right choice depends on whether your own estimate beats the implied probability. Both sides of a puck line include the sportsbook's margin. In the example, +150 and -180 imply 40.0% and 64.3%, which add up to 104.3%, a margin of about 4.1%. Removing it gives fair odds of roughly 38.4% and 61.6%; the no-vig calculator does this for any two-way line. For more on choosing between these bet types, see our moneyline betting strategy guide.

What is a run line in baseball?

The run line is baseball's spread. Caesars describes it as "fixed at ±1.5 runs": the favorite must win by 2 or more, and the underdog covers if it wins outright or loses by 1 (Caesars baseball betting guide). Like the puck line, the number rarely moves; the price does.

Example: the favorite is -160 on the moneyline and -1.5 (+130) on the run line, while the underdog is +1.5 (-150). A $100 run line bet on the favorite profits $130.00 if it wins by 2 or more, versus $62.50 on the moneyline. A $100 bet on the underdog at +1.5 (-150) profits $66.67.

Run line settlement rules to know

  • Game length: DraftKings' rules void bets on a game scheduled for nine innings if it doesn't go nine full innings, or 8.5 if the home team is leading going into the bottom of the ninth. The moneyline has its own exception. Caesars notes that run line bets "usually require the game to reach regulation" and are often refunded if it doesn't.
  • Extra innings count: DraftKings settles baseball bets, apart from individual-inning markets, including extra innings.
  • Home favorites: if the home team is ahead after the top of the ninth, it doesn't bat again, and if it takes the lead in its last at-bat the game ends as soon as the winning run scores. Both rules can cap a home favorite's margin, so think about home and away when you look at -1.5.

Rules vary by sportsbook and state, especially for shortened or suspended games, so check your book's baseball section before betting.

What does 1.5 goals mean in soccer betting?

In soccer, 1.5 goals shows up in two different markets, and it is worth knowing which one you are looking at:

  • Handicap -1.5 / +1.5: works exactly like the puck line. Team -1.5 must win by 2+; team +1.5 wins the bet with a win, a draw, or a one-goal loss. Because soccer games can end in a draw, +1.5 is a lot of cover. This is the same idea as a -1.5 Asian handicap; our Asian handicap guide covers the quarter and whole-number lines.
  • Over/under 1.5 goals: a total, not a handicap. Over 1.5 wins if the match has 2 or more goals in total (by either team); under 1.5 wins with 0 or 1 goal.

Settlement is usually on regulation time only. DraftKings' soccer rules settle bets on the score at the end of regulation "including injury and stoppage time," and leave out extra time and penalty shootouts unless the market says otherwise. So a cup tie that finishes 1-1 after 90 minutes loses a -1.5 bet even if a team wins 3-1 in extra time.

Example: a team at -1.5 priced +140 (2.40). A $50 bet profits $70.00 if it wins by 2 or more. Over 1.5 goals at -250 (1.40) profits $40.00 on a $100 bet.

What does over 1.5 mean in betting?

"Over 1.5" always means "2 or more" of whatever the market counts, and "under 1.5" means "1 or fewer." Beyond soccer goals, you will see it on totals for low-scoring periods (first-period goals in hockey) and on player props such as over 1.5 hits, over 1.5 shots on goal, or over 1.5 passing touchdowns. The half point again means there is no push.

What does -1.5 mean in football and basketball?

In the NFL, college football and the NBA, -1.5 is just a small point spread: the favorite must win by 2 or more points, and the underdog at +1.5 covers with a win or a one-point loss. Unlike hockey and baseball, the spread number changes from game to game, and a 1.5 line signals a close matchup.

These spreads are usually priced near -110 on each side. At -110 you risk $110 to win $100; a $100 bet profits $90.91, so you need to win about 52.4% of the time to break even. A tight -1.5 spread is often close to the moneyline in practice, since the favorite has to win by just 2. For key numbers and spread tactics, see our point spread betting strategy guide.

How do you calculate a 1.5 line payout?

The 1.5 decides whether you win; the odds decide how much. To work out a payout:

  1. Read the price next to the line, for example -1.5 (+150) or +1.5 (-180).
  2. For plus odds, profit = stake x odds / 100. $40 at +150 = $60.00 profit.
  3. For minus odds, profit = stake x 100 / odds. $40 at -180 = $22.22 profit.
  4. Add your stake back for the total return ($100.00 and $62.22 in these examples).
  5. Convert the odds to implied probability to see how often the bet must win: 40.0% for +150 and 64.3% for -180.

If odds conversions are new to you, our guide to understanding odds and implied probability walks through the formulas.

Common mistakes with +1.5 and -1.5 bets

  • Mixing up the line and the odds. "-1.5 (+150)" is a plus-money bet on a favorite. The first number is the margin, the second is the price.
  • Forgetting overtime and shootouts. On NHL puck lines at books such as DraftKings and Caesars, every overtime or shootout game ends with a one-goal margin, which always beats -1.5.
  • Assuming soccer extra time counts. Handicaps and goal totals are normally settled on 90 minutes plus stoppage time.
  • Treating +1.5 as a safe bet. A -180 price needs a 64.3% win rate to break even. One loss wipes out the profit from almost two wins.
  • Ignoring house rules. Game-length requirements, shortened games and international hockey rules differ by book and state.

The bottom line: -1.5 means win by 2 or more, +1.5 means don't lose by 2 or more, and the price tells you what that is worth. Before you bet either side, run the line through the spread calculator and compare its implied probability with your own estimate.
