Key takeaways

  • Minus odds show what you risk to win $100; plus odds show what $100 wins.
  • Standard -110 juice means a 4.55% hold and a 52.38% break-even rate.
  • ATS is the result against the spread; SU is the actual winner.
  • PK means no favorite; a push is a tie with the line and refunds your stake.
  • Fade means bet against someone; tail means copy their bet.
  • Settlement rules for pushes, voids and overtime vary by sportsbook, so check house rules.

Sports betting terms are the shorthand sportsbooks and bettors use to describe odds, bets and results: words like juice, chalk, ATS, PK, push and fade. Once you know about 60 of them, any bet slip, odds screen or betting podcast starts to make sense.

This glossary groups the terms by job (odds, spreads, bet types, settlement, slang and money) instead of a plain A-Z dump, and it shows the math behind the ones that cost or pay you money. By the end you will be able to read a moneyline, tell a push from a void, work out what -110 really costs you and follow betting lingo without guessing.

Quick answer: Sports betting terms are the words used to describe odds, bets and outcomes. The essentials: moneyline (pick the winner), point spread (win by a margin), total or over/under (combined score), juice or vig (the book's fee, usually -110), chalk (the favorite), PK (no favorite), push (a tie, stake refunded), and fade or tail (bet against or with someone).

What are sports betting terms, and how is this glossary organized?

Betting terms fall into a few families. Some describe the price (odds terms), some describe the line you are betting against (spread and total terms), some name the wager itself (bet types), some describe how a bet is graded (settlement terms), and the rest is betting slang and bankroll vocabulary. Learning them by family is faster than memorizing an alphabetical list, because related words explain each other.

Definitions here follow how US sportsbooks and reference glossaries use them, including the ESPN sports betting glossary and the sports betting terms list from NYProblemGambling.org. Settlement details (pushes, voids, overtime) come from published sportsbook house rules, which vary by operator and by state.

  • Odds terms: moneyline, plus and minus, juice, even money, implied probability
  • Spread and total terms: ATS, cover, PK, hook, key numbers, SU
  • Bet types: straight, parlay, teaser, round robin, props, futures
  • Settlement terms: push, void, action, grade, cash out
  • Betting slang: fade, tail, sharp, square, steam, bad beat
  • Money terms: unit, bankroll, handle, hold, CLV

What do odds terms like moneyline, juice and even money mean?

Odds tell you two things at once: how much you win and how likely the sportsbook thinks the result is. US books show American odds by default, so these are the first betting terms to learn.

  • Moneyline (ML): A bet on who wins the game outright, with no point spread. "ML" on a slip means moneyline. The Moneyline Calculator shows the payout for any price.
  • American odds (plus and minus): A minus number (-150) is how much you risk to win $100. A plus number (+150) is how much you win on a $100 stake. Minus means favorite, plus means underdog.
  • Favorite and chalk: The favorite is the side priced with a minus number. Chalk is slang for the favorite; a "chalky" card or bracket is one full of favorites.
  • Underdog (dog): The side the book rates as less likely to win, posted with a plus number on the moneyline or plus points on the spread.
  • Even money (EVEN): Odds of +100: win $100 on a $100 stake. Decimal 2.00, implied probability 50%.
  • Decimal odds: The total return per $1 staked, including your stake. -110 is about 1.91, +150 is 2.50.
  • Implied probability: The win chance built into a price. -150 implies 60.0%, +150 implies 40.0%. Our guide to odds and implied probability covers the formulas.
  • Juice, vig or vigorish: The sportsbook's fee, built into the price. On a standard -110 spread you risk $110 to win $100; the extra $10 is the juice.
  • Hold (or margin): The share of money wagered that the book expects to keep. At -110 on both sides, hold is 4.55%.
  • Odds-on: A price shorter than even money, such as -200 (decimal 1.50).

Worked example: what -110 juice really costs

Say a book takes $110 on each side of a -110/-110 spread, $220 in total. Whichever side wins collects $210 ($110 stake back plus $100 profit), so the book keeps $10. That is $10 / $220 = 4.55% hold. Each side's implied probability is 52.38%, and the two add up to 104.76% instead of 100%; the extra 4.76% is the overround. It also means you need to win 52.38% of -110 bets just to break even.

What do spread and total terms like ATS, PK and the hook mean?

Point spreads and totals are how books balance lopsided games. These betting terms come up constantly in NFL, college football and NBA coverage.

  • Point spread (the line): A head start given to the underdog. Bears +6.5 means the Bears' score gets 6.5 points added for grading; Packers -6.5 means the Packers must win by 7 or more. Try any line in the Spread Calculator.
  • ATS (against the spread): A result or record measured against the spread instead of the scoreboard. A team that is "8-3 ATS" covered in 8 of its 11 games, whether or not it won them.
  • SU (straight up): The actual winner, ignoring the spread. A 3-point underdog that loses by 1 lost SU but won ATS.
  • Cover: To beat the spread. A favorite covers by winning by more than the number; an underdog covers by winning outright or losing by less than the number.
  • Laying points and taking points: Betting the favorite is laying the points. Betting the underdog is taking the points.
  • PK or pick'em: A game with no favorite: the spread is 0, so you just need your team to win. If a pick'em game ends tied, which can happen in the NFL regular season, the margin lands exactly on 0 and the bet is graded as a push.
  • Hook: A half point on a spread or total, such as -7.5. A 7.5-point favorite is "laying seven and the hook." The hook exists to prevent pushes. The Half-Point Calculator shows what buying or selling a hook is worth.
  • Key numbers: The most common winning margins in a sport. In the NFL, 3 and 7 stand out because of field goals and touchdowns, so moving a spread across 3 or 7 matters far more than moving it from 5 to 5.5.
  • Total or over/under (O/U): A bet on the combined score. Over 44.5 wins if both teams score 45 or more combined. Our guide to what over/under means covers totals in every sport.
  • Puck line and run line: The standard 1.5-goal hockey spread and 1.5-run baseball spread, explained in what -1.5 means in betting.
  • Backdoor cover: A late, often meaningless score that flips the spread result, such as a trailing team's last-minute touchdown turning a 10-point loss into a 3-point loss.
  • Alternate lines (alt lines): Extra spreads or totals at different numbers and prices: a bigger spread pays more, a smaller one pays less.

Which bet types should every bettor know?

These are the wagers you will see on every US sportsbook menu. Availability and rules vary by book and state.

  • Straight bet (single): one wager on one outcome, such as a moneyline, spread or total.
  • Parlay: two or more legs combined into one ticket; every leg must win, and the odds multiply. Three -110 legs pay about 5.96 to 1. Price one in the Parlay Calculator and read our parlay betting tips.
  • Same game parlay (SGP): a parlay whose legs all come from one game. Books price these themselves because the legs are related.
  • Teaser: a football or basketball parlay where each spread or total moves in your favor by a fixed number of points, for a lower payout. See what a teaser bet is.
  • Round robin: a set of smaller parlays built from one list of picks, so one loss does not sink everything. See how round robins work.
  • Prop (proposition) bet: a bet on something other than the final result, such as a player's passing yards or the first team to score.
  • Futures: a bet on an outcome decided later, like a championship winner or a team's season win total.
  • Live or in-game betting: wagers placed after the event starts, with odds that update as play goes on.
  • Three-way moneyline (1X2): a market with a draw option, common in soccer and in regulation-time hockey bets.

What do push, void, action and grading mean when a bet settles?

Settlement terms decide whether you get paid, refunded or nothing at all. The exact rules live in each sportsbook's house rules, so treat these as the common pattern rather than a promise.

  • Push: A tie with the line. A team favored by 3 wins by exactly 3, or a total of 47 lands on 47. On a straight bet your stake is refunded. MGM's Massachusetts house rules state it plainly: "All wagers resulting in a push will be refunded."
  • Void or no action: The bet is cancelled and the stake returned, for example when a game is postponed past the book's deadline or a listed pitcher does not start.
  • Action: Any live wager, or the amount bet on an event ("there's a lot of action on the over"). In baseball, an "action" bet stands no matter who pitches, while a "listed pitchers" bet is cancelled if either listed starter does not start.
  • Grade (or settle): When the book marks a bet as a win, loss, push or void and pays it out.
  • Cash out: An offer from the book to settle your bet early for a set amount. The book sets that price, so compare it with the hedge value in the Hedging Calculator before you accept.
  • Overtime rules: Full-game lines usually include overtime and extra innings, but markets that offer a tie or draw as an option are usually graded on regulation only. Check the market rules before betting.

Worked example: a push inside a parlay

You bet $10 on a three-leg parlay, each leg at -110. If all three win, the ticket pays $69.58 ($59.58 profit). Now say one leg pushes. At most books that leg is removed and the parlay is repriced as a two-leg ticket: $36.45 back, or $26.45 profit. Some books handle pushes in same game parlays or teasers differently, so check the house rules for that bet type.

What does betting slang like fade, tail, sharp and square mean?

Sports betting slang and lingo show up on social media, podcasts and betting shows. None of these words appear on a bet slip, but they explain how bettors talk about each other.

  • Fade (fading): Betting against a team, player, tipster or the public. "Fading the public" means taking the side most casual bettors avoid.
  • Tail (tailing): Copying someone else's bet. If a friend posts a pick and you place the same wager, you tailed it. Tailing anyone still leaves the risk on you.
  • Sharp: A professional or highly skilled bettor. "Sharp money" is betting volume the book respects enough to move a line.
  • Square (public or rec bettor): A casual or recreational bettor, the opposite of a sharp. "Public money" is the volume from recreational bettors.
  • Steam (steam move): A line that moves quickly and sharply, usually across many books at once, because of heavy betting or breaking news.
  • Bad beat: Losing a bet that looked won late in the game, often on a backdoor cover or a last-second score.
  • Mush: Slang for a bettor (or a pick) that seems to bring bad luck.
  • Handicapper (capper): Someone who analyzes games and sells or shares picks.
  • Hit rate: The percentage of bets that win. At -110 you need a 52.4% hit rate to break even, so a winning record alone does not prove profit.

What do unit, bankroll, handle and stake mean?

These terms describe money: yours and the sportsbook's. Getting them right is the difference between "I won" and "I made money."

  • Stake (risk or wager): The amount you bet.
  • To win vs payout: "To win" is your profit. "Payout" or "return" is profit plus your stake. A $110 bet at -110 is "to win $100" with a $210 payout. The Betting Calculator shows both.
  • Bankroll: The money you have set aside for betting and can afford to lose.
  • Unit (1u): A standard bet size, usually a fixed share of your bankroll. With a $1,000 bankroll and 1% units, 1u is $10 and 2u is $20. Our guide to bankroll management covers how to size units, and the Kelly Criterion Calculator shows a math-based alternative.
  • Handle: The total amount of money a sportsbook accepts on a game, an event or over a period. Handle is not profit.
  • Hold: What the book keeps from its handle after paying winners.
  • ROI (return on investment): Profit divided by total amount wagered. Track yours with the ROI Calculator.
  • EV (expected value): The average profit or loss a bet would produce if you could repeat it many times. Positive EV (+EV) means the price is better than the true odds. Check a bet in the Expected Value Calculator.
  • Opening line, closing line and line movement: The first price posted, the last price before the game starts, and the changes in between.
  • CLV (closing line value): Getting a better price than the closing line. Betting -3 when the game closes at -4.5 is positive CLV.
  • Line shopping: Comparing the same bet at several legal books to get the best price.
  • Off the board (OTB): A game the book has stopped taking bets on, often because of injury news.
  • Limit: The maximum stake a book will accept on a market.

How do these betting terms fit together on a real bet slip?

Here is how the vocabulary reads on a typical NFL Sunday ticket. Suppose the Chiefs are -3 (-110) against the Bills, the total is 47.5 and the moneylines are Chiefs -150 and Bills +130.

  1. Spread: backing the Chiefs is laying 3 points. They are the favorite, or chalk. A 3-point Chiefs win is a push on the spread: stake refunded.
  2. Juice: -110 means a $55 bet wins $50. Break-even is 52.38%.
  3. Moneyline: Chiefs -150 implies 60.0%; Bills +130 implies 43.5%. Together that is 103.48%, so the moneyline hold is about 3.36%. With the vig removed, the fair probabilities are about 58.0% and 42.0%.
  4. Total: 47.5 has a hook, so it cannot push. 48 or more total points wins the over.
  5. Slang: if most of the public is on the Chiefs and you take the Bills +3, you are fading the public and taking the points.

Which sports betting terms do beginners mix up most?

  • Payout vs profit: a "payout" includes your stake. A $100 bet at +150 pays $250, but the profit is $150.
  • ATS vs SU: a team can lose straight up and still cover against the spread.
  • Push vs void: both return your stake, but a push means the game finished on the number, while a void means the bet was cancelled.
  • Juice vs hold: juice is the fee in a single price; hold is the share of all money bet that the book keeps.
  • Handle vs revenue: big handle numbers in the news are amounts wagered, not sportsbook profits.
  • Units vs dollars: "up 5 units" means nothing until you know the unit size.
  • Chalk vs certainty: favorites lose regularly. Implied probability, not the word "favorite," tells you how often the book expects them to win.

Frequently Asked Questions

PK stands for pick'em: a game with no favorite and a point spread of 0. You only need your team to win. If the game ends tied, the margin lands exactly on 0 and the spread bet is graded as a push, so your stake is refunded. Pick'em spreads usually still carry juice, such as -110 on both sides.
Chalk is slang for the favorite, the side priced with a minus number. Betting chalk means backing favorites, and a chalky slate is one where most favorites win. Chalk is never certain: a -200 favorite implies a 66.7% chance, so the book expects it to lose about one time in three.
A push is a tie with the betting line, such as a 3-point favorite winning by exactly 3 or a total of 47 landing on 47. On a straight bet the stake is refunded. In a regular parlay, the pushed leg is usually removed and the ticket is repriced with the remaining legs, though rules vary by book.
ATS means against the spread. It describes a result or record measured against the point spread instead of the final score. A team that is 6-4 ATS covered the spread in six of ten games. A team can lose straight up and still win ATS if it loses by less than the spread.
Fading means betting against a team, player, tipster or the betting public. If a popular bettor likes the over and you bet the under, you are fading them. Fading the public is a common phrase for taking the side recreational bettors avoid; it is a style, not a winning system on its own.
Tailing means copying someone else's bet, usually a pick shared on social media or by a friend. You place the same wager, ideally at the same or a better price. Line movement after a pick is posted can change the value, so check the current odds with the Odds Converter before you tail.
SU (straight up) is who actually won the game. ATS (against the spread) is who won after the point spread is applied. If the Packers are 7-point favorites and win 24-20, they won SU but lost ATS, and Bears +7 bettors won their bet.
Handle is the total amount of money wagered with a sportsbook on a game, an event or over a period such as a month. It is not the book's profit. What the book keeps after paying winners is called hold or revenue, which is a small fraction of handle.
