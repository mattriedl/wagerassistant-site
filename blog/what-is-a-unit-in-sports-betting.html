What Is a Unit in Sports Betting? How Units Work, With Examples

Key takeaways

  • A unit is your standard bet size, usually 1% to 5% of your betting bankroll.
  • 1u means one standard bet; 5u means five times your standard bet.
  • Count units won at the real odds: 1u at -110 wins 0.91u, 1u at +150 wins 1.5u.
  • Pair your unit total with ROI, because a winning record can still lose units.
  • Keep your unit size fixed and only change it after a planned bankroll review.

A unit in sports betting is your standard bet size, a fixed amount of money that usually equals 1% to 5% of your betting bankroll. If your bankroll is $1,000 and you choose a 2% unit, one unit (written "1u") is $20, and every bet you place is sized as a multiple of that number.

Units let bettors talk about results without revealing dollar amounts. "Up 5 units" means the same thing whether someone bets $10 or $1,000 per play. In this guide you will learn what units mean, how much a unit should be, how units won and lost are calculated at different odds, and how to track your own record in units with real math.

Quick answer: A unit is a bettor's standard stake, usually 1% to 5% of their bankroll. With a $1,000 bankroll and a 1% unit, 1u is $10 and 3u is $30. Results are then tracked in units, so a bettor who is "+4.5u" is ahead 4.5 times their standard bet, whatever that is in dollars.

What is a unit in sports betting?

A unit is a measuring stick for bet size. BetMGM describes it as "a basic amount of money that a handicapper will regularly risk on a standard bet". You pick the dollar value once, based on your bankroll, and then size every wager in units instead of thinking about raw dollars.

Two terms matter here:

  • Bankroll: the money you have set aside only for betting, separate from rent, bills and savings.
  • Unit size: the dollar value of one unit, usually a small percentage of that bankroll.

So when you see a bettor on social media post "2u on the over," they are saying the bet is twice their normal size. It does not tell you how many dollars they risked, and that is the point. The Action Network sums it up as a way to compare win amounts between bettors while removing money from the conversation.

If you are newer to betting vocabulary, our sports betting terms glossary covers related words like stake, handle and bankroll.

How much is a unit in betting?

There is no official unit size. It depends entirely on your bankroll and how much risk you want. Most published guidance lands in the same low range: BetMGM mentions 1% as a common choice, with about 0.5% for conservative bettors and about 2.5% for aggressive ones, while the bettingexpert glossary says common advice suggests 1-5% of your bankroll per bet.

Here is what those percentages look like in dollars:

Bankroll1% unit2% unit3% unit5% unit
$500$5$10$15$25
$1,000$10$20$30$50
$2,500$25$50$75$125
$5,000$50$100$150$250

Smaller units mean slower swings. With a flat 1% unit, ten straight one-unit losses cost 10% of your starting bankroll. With a flat 5% unit, the same ten losses cost half of it. Losing streaks of that length happen to everyone eventually, even on bets near a coin flip, which is why most guides lean toward the low end.

What do 1u, 0.5u and 5 units mean in betting?

The number in front of the "u" is a multiple of your unit. Using a $20 unit as the example:

ShorthandMeaningStake with a $20 unit
0.5uHalf-unit bet, often used for long shots or parlays$10
1uA standard bet$20
2uDouble the standard bet$40
5uFive times the standard bet$100
+5.2uA running result: ahead 5.2 units overall$104 profit
-3uA running result: down 3 units overall$60 loss

"What does 5 units mean in betting?" usually comes up when a tout or a friend posts a "5-unit play." That simply means the bet is five times their normal size. A 5u bet at a 2% unit puts 10% of the bankroll on one game, which is a big swing for a single result. Keep that in mind before copying anyone's sizing.

How are units won and lost calculated?

A unit describes how much you risk, but the odds decide how much you win. That is where most confusion comes from. Under the most common convention, you risk 1 unit and your profit depends on the price:

  • Negative (favorite) odds: profit = units risked x 100 / odds. At -110, 1u wins 0.91u.
  • Positive (underdog) odds: profit = units risked x odds / 100. At +150, 1u wins 1.5u.
  • A loss always costs exactly what you risked: -1u.
  • A push returns your stake: 0u.

The Action Network uses the same math in its example: 1 unit on a -110 spread bet wins about .91 units if the team covers. You can check any of these payouts with our Betting Calculator or, for moneylines, the Moneyline Calculator.

Risk units vs to-win units

Some bettors use a second convention: on favorites they bet "to win" 1 unit instead of risking 1 unit. At -110 that means risking 1.1u to win 1u. Both methods are used, which is why two records can look different even with identical picks. Here is how the two compare:

Neither convention is wrong, but whichever you choose, use it every time and say which one you use when you share results. The gap between the two columns is the bookmaker's built-in margin at work; our guide to what vig is explains why a -110 bet pays less than even money.

How do you track a betting record in units? (worked example)

Say your bankroll is $1,000 and your unit is 2%, so 1u = $20. You place six bets over a weekend using the risk convention:

BetOddsUnits riskedResultUnitsRunning totalDollars
Point spread-1101uWin+0.91u+0.91u+$18.18
Underdog moneyline+1501uLoss-1.00u-0.09u-$20.00
Game total-1051uWin+0.95u+0.86u+$19.05
Favorite moneyline-2002uWin+1.00u+1.86u+$20.00
2-leg parlay+2640.5uLoss-0.50u+1.36u-$10.00
Point spread-1101uPush0.00u+1.36u$0.00

The weekend finishes at 3-2-1 (wins, losses, pushes) and +1.36 units, which is a profit of $27.23 at $20 per unit. You risked 6.5 units in total, so your return on investment (ROI) is 1.36 / 6.5 = 20.9%. (The 2-leg parlay price comes from two -110 legs: 1.9091 x 1.9091 = 3.6446 in decimal odds, or about +264.)

Notice how the win-loss record alone hides what happened. The 2u favorite at -200 earned only 1 unit, while the single +150 loss cost a full unit. Units plus ROI tell the real story. Our ROI Calculator does this math for a longer record.

Why do bettors use units instead of dollars?

  • Fair comparisons. A $10 bettor and a $500 bettor who make the same picks at the same odds have the same unit record.
  • Privacy. You can share results without revealing how much money you bet.
  • Discipline. A fixed unit stops you from betting $200 on a game just because it is on national TV.
  • Honest tracking. Units, combined with ROI, show whether your bets are profitable once the odds are taken into account. At standard -110 pricing you need to win 52.4% of your bets just to break even, so a winning record can still lose units.

For example, a 55-45 record on 100 one-unit bets at -110 works out to 55 x 0.91 - 45 = +5.0 units, a 5.0% ROI. The same 55-45 record at worse prices could be close to zero. Check break-even rates for any price with the Implied Probability Calculator.

How do you choose your betting unit size?

  1. Set your bankroll first. Use only money you can afford to lose. The National Council on Problem Gambling advises bettors to determine a budget before placing any bets and never to use funds meant for rent, bills or savings.
  2. Pick a percentage. 1% to 2% is a common starting point; going above 5% makes losing streaks very painful.
  3. Do the math once. Bankroll x percentage = unit. $800 x 1.5% = $12 per unit.
  4. Decide fixed or recalculated. Flat staking keeps the same dollar unit until you choose to reset it. Percentage staking recalculates the unit from your current bankroll, so it shrinks after losses (ten 5% losses in a row leave about 59.9% of the bankroll, compared with 50% under flat 5% bets).
  5. Set a rule for bigger bets. If you ever bet more than 1u, decide in advance why, and cap it (for example, never more than 2u).
  6. Track every bet in units, including the odds, so your ROI is accurate.

What unit mistakes should you avoid?

  • Changing your unit size to protect a record. The Action Network warns that losing several small bets and then dropping one huge "hammer" is a way to misrepresent a record. For example, four 1u losses followed by one 5u win at -110 nets just +0.55u, even though it is a 1-4 record that "won big" on the last game.
  • Ignoring the odds. Ten wins on -250 favorites are not worth the same as ten wins at +150. Always count units at the actual price.
  • Quoting records without ROI. "+20 units" over 2,000 one-unit bets is a 1% ROI; over 100 one-unit bets it is 20%.
  • Raising your unit while chasing losses. Increase it only after a planned bankroll review, never mid-streak.
  • Copying someone else's unit sizing. A tout's 5u play may be a small bet for them and a huge one for you.
  • Mixing conventions. Switching between risk and to-win units makes your record impossible to compare over time.

The bottom line on betting units

A unit is just your standard bet, set as a small, fixed share of a bankroll you can afford to lose. Size every bet as a multiple of it, count wins at the real odds, and pair your unit total with ROI so the number means something.
